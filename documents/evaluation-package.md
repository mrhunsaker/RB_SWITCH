**Using the RB Switch in these trials**

The RB Switch has three small mechanical buttons: SW1 sends Left Arrow, SW2 sends Enter, and SW3 sends Right Arrow. How it fits each stage depends on the host device and app, so confirm the behavior in the actual app before you start collecting data.

| Package stage | How the RB Switch can be used |
|---|---|
| Stage 1 and 2: cause and effect, purposeful activation | Use any one button as the switch. Check that the app (or the Switch Control / Switch Access action you assign to the button) responds before collecting data. |
| Stage 3: single-switch scanning | Assign one button to **Select** and turn on auto scanning in iOS Switch Control or Android Switch Access. Record the scan speed on the data sheet. |
| Stage 4: two-switch step scanning | Use SW3 as **Next** (advance) and SW2 as **Select**. This is the two-switch setup Android's wizard supports. |
| Stage 5: multi-item and AAC | Use all three buttons: SW1 **Previous**, SW3 **Next**, SW2 **Select**. On Android, the third button is added after the wizard. |

Keep these limits in mind when you complete Section 4 (Motor & Access Site Assessment):

- The RB Switch buttons are small tactile buttons that need a deliberate press. It is one option among the switch types in Section 4.4 and does not replace large-plate, pillow, proximity, sip-and-puff, or other sensor switches for students who need them.
- It connects only by Bluetooth and has no jack for external switches.
- Record the RB Switch (and how it is mounted or positioned) as the switch type and placement on each data sheet.

## SECTION 1: Background & Theoretical Framework

### 1.1 What Are Adaptive Switches?

Adaptive switches are alternative access devices that allow individuals with physical, cognitive, and/or sensory disabilities to control technology without using standard touch or keyboard input. A switch is essentially a simple on/off interface—when activated (pressed, squeezed, blown into, moved toward, etc.), it sends a signal to a device, completing an action selected by scanning software or cause-effect applications.

For students with cerebral palsy (CP) and cortical/cerebral visual impairment (CVI), adaptive switches can unlock access to communication, learning, play, and environmental control that would otherwise be inaccessible.

The switch itself is not the intervention; it is the access mechanism that connects the person's response to a meaningful activity or outcome.

#### The Access Chain

Effective switch access can be understood as a chain with six linked components:

1. **Person** — the individual's motor, sensory, visual, cognitive, communication, and motivational characteristics.
2. **Response** — a voluntary movement or other observable action the person can perform with useful reliability.
3. **Switch** — the sensor or interface that detects the response without requiring unnecessary force, range, speed, or precision.
4. **Mounting and positioning** — the physical relationship among the person, switch, seating system, and activity.
5. **Interface or software** — the system that interprets the switch signal through cause-effect, timed activation, scanning, selection, AAC, or computer access.
6. **Outcome** — a meaningful consequence such as communication, social interaction, preferred stimulation, learning, play, environmental control, or task completion.

> **Important — Training principle:** If performance is poor, do not assume the student needs more prompting. Examine every link in the access chain before increasing assistance.

#### Switch Use vs. Switch Access

A student can physically press a switch without demonstrating functional switch use. Functional access requires a relationship between the response, the timing of the response, the selected target, and the meaningful outcome. Intentionality—not simple physical activation—determines readiness to advance through the stages.

#### Continuum of Switch Use: From Cause-Effect to Multi-Item Scanning

> **Important — Key Principle:** The progression from cause-effect to scanning is NOT linear in a strict developmental sense—it is an instructional sequence shaped by cognitive readiness, motor access, and visual processing. Students should move to the next level when they demonstrate consistent intentionality, NOT simply when they can physically activate a switch. A student may require one stage for one activity while using a more advanced method for another.

The instructional hierarchy for switch access typically proceeds through five stages:

- **Stage 1 – Cause and Effect:** Student activates a switch and experiences a contingent outcome (sound, light, movement). Goal is understanding that their action creates change in the environment.
- **Stage 2 – Purposeful Activation:** Student activates a switch at specific moments (e.g., to turn music on/off, answer yes/no, operate a toy). There is a clear communicative or operational intent.
- **Stage 3 – Single Switch Scanning:** An on-screen cursor or highlight moves through items automatically (auto-scan) or with a 'next' switch press (step scan). The student activates the switch to select the highlighted item.
- **Stage 4 – Two-Switch Scanning:** One switch advances the scan; a second switch selects. This approach reduces accidental selections and allows finer control. Students develop the ability to wait, anticipate, and time their selections.
- **Stage 5 – Multiple Switch / Directed Scanning:** Multiple switches navigate in multiple directions (up, down, left, right, select). Used for complex grids, AAC systems, and computer control.

| Stage | Primary skill | Typical access arrangement | Primary evidence of learning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Cause and effect | Action produces a predictable consequence | One switch + contingent stimulus | Repeated independent activations tied to the outcome |
| 2. Purposeful/timed activation | Action occurs at a meaningful moment | One switch + structured opportunity | Timing and communicative/operational intent |
| 3. Single-switch scanning | One switch selects among sequential choices | Auto-scan or step scan | Accurate selection of targets |
| 4. Two-switch scanning | Separate advance and select responses | Two switches | Deliberate navigation and selection |
| 5. Multi-item/AAC integration | Navigation, selection, and message construction | Multiple switches or scanning | Functional communication/control across contexts |

#### Why Students with CP and CVI Require Special Consideration

Cerebral palsy affects motor control in ways that range from minor coordination difficulties to complete absence of voluntary limb movement. Students may have spasticity, athetosis, ataxia, or mixed types, each affecting the precision, timing, and consistency of switch activation differently. CP can also affect tone, range of motion, motor planning, timing, strength, coordination, endurance, and movement consistency—so switch selection must be individualized to the student's specific presentation. A site-by-site motor assessment is essential before any switch trials.

Cortical/Cerebral Visual Impairment (CVI) is a neurological condition affecting how the brain processes visual information. It is the leading cause of visual impairment in children in developed countries and frequently co-occurs with CP due to shared neurological etiology. CVI manifests along a spectrum (Roman-Lantzy CVI Range: Phase I–III) and profoundly affects:

- Color preference (high-contrast, single colors may be required)
- Visual field preferences (lateral, lower, or central depending on phase)
- Need for movement to attract and sustain visual attention
- Visual latency (delayed processing—student may not respond immediately)
- Complexity tolerance (clutter, backgrounds, and competing visual stimuli impair access)
- Distance vision and near vision variability

These visual processing characteristics directly affect switch interface design—apps, overlays, scanning highlights, icon size, contrast, and background must be calibrated to the student's CVI phase.

Visual impairment—including blindness—does not automatically prevent switch use. It changes how the evaluator should establish orientation to the task, feedback, target presentation, auditory cues, and scanning.

### 1.2 Evidence Base

The use of switch-based assistive technology is supported by a robust evidence base across multiple disciplines:

- **HAAT model:** Switch access framed within the Human Activity Assistive Technology model, emphasizing that technology efficacy depends on alignment between the student's abilities, the activity demands, and the assistive technology context.
- **Microswitch research:** Demonstrated that even micro-switch responses can establish meaningful communication and environmental control for students with the most profound disabilities.
- **CVI Range:** Provides a standardized observational instrument for measuring CVI characteristics and guides environmental modifications directly applicable to switch interface design.
- **SETT Framework:** Student-Environment-Tasks-Tools—the gold standard for AT evaluation, requiring assessment of student needs in the context of their specific environments and tasks before selecting tools.
- **QIAT:** Provides standards for AT evaluation, implementation, and follow-up in educational settings.
- **AAC research:** Demonstrates that switch-based access is a viable and effective pathway to augmentative communication for students with complex communication needs.

These sources are complementary rather than interchangeable: SETT and HAAT organize feature matching and context; QIAT addresses service delivery; CVI assessment informs visual access; AAC research addresses communication outcomes; and microswitch studies provide examples of people with severe or profound disabilities learning to control meaningful environmental or social events.

> **Warning — Evidence limitation:** Most microswitch research is based on small samples and single-case or case-series designs. It is highly useful for demonstrating feasibility and functional relationships in individuals with complex disabilities, but it does not establish a universal switch type, scan speed, or success rate for all students.

### 1.3 Functional Outcomes

Switch access should ultimately serve functional outcomes such as:

- Requesting preferred objects, activities, people, or repetitions
- Rejecting, stopping, or protesting an activity
- Answering yes/no questions
- Selecting between two or more options
- Accessing music, videos, toys, lights, vibration, or other preferred stimulation
- Operating environmental controls when appropriate and safely configured
- Participating in AAC through scanning
- Accessing computer or tablet functions
- Increasing active engagement in educational, recreational, social, or occupational activities

### 2.5 SETT Framework Application

Apply the SETT framework before selecting tools. Define the student by functional characteristics, not diagnosis or GMFCS/MACS level alone.

**Student** — Document the student's motor repertoire, voluntary movement, tone, range, endurance, communication, cognition, sensory profile, vision, hearing, visual latency, preferences, and typical patterns of initiation.

**Environment** — Identify where switch access must work: classroom, therapy, cafeteria, playground, home, community, transportation, and other relevant environments. Record seating systems, table height, device location, lighting, noise, people moving behind the display, caregiver availability, and device-management demands.

**Tasks** — Specify the real activity. "Use switch" is not a functional task. Examples include "request continuation of a song," "select a preferred story," "answer yes/no during a classroom routine," "call caregiver," or "select a word in AAC."

**Tools** — Select the least complex tool that provides reliable access to the required task. Consider switch type, activation force, travel, sensitivity, mounting, cable routing, connector, interface, scan mode, scan speed, feedback, and durability.

### 3.4 Avoiding False Conclusions

> **Warning:** A low activation rate can result from poor positioning, excessive switch force, poor switch placement, inadequate motivation, excessive visual complexity, insufficient wait time, fatigue, or an inappropriate response criterion. Conversely, a high activation rate can reflect accidental contact or a movement that is not under intentional control. Analyze the contingency—do not just count switch presses.

**Positioning sequence:**

1. Establish the safest and most functional seating posture.
2. Stabilize the trunk and pelvis as clinically appropriate.
3. Establish head support or independent head control as needed.
4. Place feet and lower limbs so that unintended contact does not activate the switch.
5. Position the activity and screen within the student's usable visual, auditory, and motor field.
6. Place the switch at the point where the target movement can occur with minimal effort and maximal consistency.
7. Recheck the position after several trials—movement, tone, and fatigue can change during the session.

**Initial switch-fit protocol:**

1. Observe natural voluntary movement.
2. Select two or three candidate responses.
3. Choose a switch capable of detecting each response with minimal physical burden.
4. Stabilize seating and mount the switch.
5. Test 10 opportunities with a preferred consequence.
6. Measure independent activation, latency, false activation, and fatigue.
7. Repeat with the next candidate site.
8. Compare functional performance, not just raw activation frequency.

### 4.4 Switch Type Selection Guide

- **Large plate switches** — Tolerate broad contact; useful when the student can produce a gross push but cannot isolate a finger. Often appropriate early in training or when motor variability is substantial.
- **Small button switches** — Provide a precise target for students with isolated finger or hand movements. Do not select simply because the student can touch a small object; the movement must be repeatable and intentional.
- **Pillow and cushion switches** — Useful for broad movements and for students who need to contact a surface rather than reach a discrete button.
- **Wobble and position switches** — Translate directional movement into a switch event. Especially useful when the target movement is head, arm, or body displacement rather than a forceful press.
- **Proximity switches** — Detect movement toward a sensor and may reduce the force requirement. The primary evaluation issue is specificity: the sensor must distinguish intentional movement from routine posture shifts.
- **Sip-and-puff** — Provide one or more discrete inputs when breath control is sufficiently reliable. Requires careful attention to tubing placement, hygiene, respiratory considerations, and the distinction between intentional and incidental breath events.
- **Micro-switches** — May refer to a small physical switch or, in the research literature, to a sensor system designed to detect a very small response (pressure, position, optical, or camera-based). The engineering goal is reliable detection of the person's response, not merely use of a commercially labeled 'micro' button.

### 4.5 Feature-Matching Matrix

| Observed need | Potential feature | What to test | Data to collect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Very small movement | Micro/low-force switch or proximity sensor | Reliable detection without accidental activation | Activation reliability, false activations, fatigue |
| Large, imprecise movement | Large plate or cushion switch | Whether the switch tolerates variable contact location | Successful activations / opportunities |
| Head movement | Wobble, proximity, position sensor | Right/left/forward/back response | Range, consistency, latency |
| Limited hand use | Palm, finger, or button switch | Contact area and force | Independent activation percentage |
| Foot/knee response | Large plate or pedal-like switch | Alignment and leg movement | Accuracy, fatigue, posture |
| Minimal visual access | Auditory feedback and simplified display | Response without visual clutter | Correct selection, latency |
| CVI with visual latency | Extended wait time | 5+ second response window as appropriate | Latency and correct response rate |
| AAC scanning | Auto-scan or step scan | Several scan speeds and array sizes | Accuracy, latency, errors, fatigue |

Students with CVI require deliberate, phase-matched interface design. This section documents the specific visual parameters that must be incorporated into all switch-access materials and digital interfaces.

Visual access is separate from motor access: a student may have an excellent motor response but still perform poorly if the interface cannot be visually located or discriminated. Conversely, a student with very limited useful vision may perform well when the task is primarily auditory or tactile.

**Phase I Characteristics (Score 0–3) — Interface Adaptations Required:**

| Characteristic | Interface Adaptation |
| --- | --- |
| Strong color preference (yellow, red) | Use single preferred color for switch & screen elements; black background |
| Need for movement to attract/hold gaze | Animated targets; moving switch indicator; lights on switch |
| Latent visual response (>3 second delay) | Extend inter-trial interval; do not interpret latency as lack of response |
| Visual field preference (typically lateral) | Position screen and switch in preferred field |
| Near viewing preference | Screen within 8–12 inches; switch within field of view |
| Single target presentation | One item on screen at a time; no competing visual elements |
| Non-purposeful gaze / reflexive looking | Do not score reflexive glances—require sustained gaze (2+ sec) |

**Phase II–III Characteristics (Score 4–10) — Interface Adaptations:**

| Characteristic | Interface Adaptation |
| --- | --- |
| Emerging color recognition (multiple colors) | Can introduce 2–3 color options; still keep high contrast |
| Increasing complexity tolerance (Phase III) | Gradually introduce more items per screen; monitor fatigue |
| Improved visual field range | Expand item placement; test central vs. peripheral placement |
| Reduced visual latency | Can slightly reduce inter-trial interval; still monitor for delays |
| Emerging distance vision | Gradually increase viewing distance; test at 18" and 24" |
| Visual fatigue remains a concern | Build in visual rest periods; limit high-visual-demand tasks |

### 5.1 CVI Variables to Document

| Variable | Assessment question | Possible training adjustment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Color | Is one color more consistently detected? | Use the preferred color for targets/highlights and reduce competing colors. |
| Visual field | Does the student respond more reliably in a particular field? | Place the screen/switch and important feedback in the usable field. |
| Movement | Does movement attract or sustain visual attention? | Use purposeful, limited animation rather than decorative motion. |
| Latency | How long does the student need to respond? | Increase wait time before prompting or advancing. |
| Complexity | How many items can be presented without loss of access? | Reduce array size and background clutter. |
| Distance | What viewing distance supports the strongest response? | Adjust screen/switch distance and gradually generalize. |
| Fatigue | Does visual performance decline over time? | Use scheduled rest and shorter high-demand trials. |

### 12.4 Interpreting Trajectories

Look at the trajectory rather than a single percentage. A student who moves from 20% to 40% to 65% may be demonstrating meaningful learning even if the formal criterion has not been met. A student who moves from 95% to 70% to 50% may require investigation of fatigue, mounting, motivation, or interface changes.

### 12.5 Decision Rules

| Finding | Possible interpretation | Next action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High accuracy + low prompts | Access may be well matched | Generalize and increase functional complexity cautiously |
| High accuracy + high prompts | Skill may be prompt-dependent | Fade prompts and strengthen natural cues |
| Low accuracy + consistent early selections | Timing may be too fast or target anticipation weak | Test slower scan speed and longer response window |
| Low accuracy + no activations | Switch/site may be physically inaccessible or motivation low | Reassess access site, force, positioning, and preference |
| High activation + low correct selection | Possible accidental or impulsive activation | Change activation requirement, scan mode, or switch placement |
| Performance drops with duration | Fatigue or visual load may be limiting | Shorten sessions, add rest, or modify visual demands |

### 12.6 Structured-to-Natural Progression & Generalization

**Progression sequence:**

1. Establish the response under highly controlled conditions.
2. Reduce prompts while maintaining the same access method.
3. Vary activity while keeping the switch parameters stable.
4. Vary communication partner or staff member.
5. Vary setting and time of day.
6. Introduce naturally occurring opportunities.
7. Monitor maintenance after a period without direct teaching.

**Generalization matrix:**

| Dimension | Example variation | Criterion to consider |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Person | Teacher → paraprofessional → parent | Independent response with natural cue |
| Activity | Music → classroom choice → leisure | Same access response serves meaningful functions |
| Setting | Classroom → home → community | Performance remains usable after environmental change |
| Time | Morning → afternoon | Performance maintained despite fatigue differences |
| Array | 2 → 4 → 8 items | Accuracy remains acceptable as complexity increases |
| Response | Same movement with different outcomes | Student understands functional contingency |

## APPENDIX A: Prompt Hierarchy Reference

| Code | Prompt Type | Description | Fading Goal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **PP** | Physical – Full | Hand-over-hand or full body guidance to complete the action | Fade to partial physical |
| **PP-** | Physical – Partial | Light touch, gestural touch to initiate movement | Fade to model |
| **M** | Model | Evaluator demonstrates the action; student imitates | Fade to gesture |
| **G** | Gesture | Point, nod, or look toward switch without touch or verbalization | Fade to verbal |
| **V** | Verbal | Spoken direction: 'activate your switch' or 'push' | Fade to natural cue |
| **NC** | Natural Cue | Environmental cue only (e.g., item appears on screen) | Target level |
| **I** | Independent | No prompt—student self-initiates in response to natural cue | Goal level |

**Avoiding prompt dependency:**

- Use a consistent prompt hierarchy.
- Allow adequate latency before prompting.
- Do not repeat verbal prompts rapidly.
- Record the highest level of assistance actually required.
- Plan prompt fading before teaching begins.
- Test whether the student can respond after the natural cue alone.
- Generalize to different adults so the response is not controlled by one person's voice or movement.

## APPENDIX D: IEP Goal Writing Bank — Adaptive Switch Access

> **Tip — How to use:** Goals below are written using the format: Condition + Student will + Behavior + Criterion + Generalization/Consistency. Replace bracketed placeholders with student-specific information.

### Stage 1 – Cause and Effect

*Given a [preferred sensory stimulus / cause-effect app] presented at [distance] with [CVI adaptations], [Student] will activate [switch type] at [primary access site] to produce a contingent outcome independently (without physical prompting) in 8 out of 10 trials across 3 consecutive data collection sessions, as measured by direct observation data.*

### Stage 2 – Purposeful / Timed Activation

*Given a structured [yes/no / turn-taking / requesting] opportunity using a [switch type] mounted at [site], [Student] will activate the switch at the appropriate time (within [x] seconds of the communicative opportunity) to indicate [choice/request/protest] with 80% accuracy across 3 consecutive sessions in at least 2 different activities, as measured by teacher/therapist data sheets.*

### Stage 3 – Single Switch Scanning

*Given an iPad application with [number]-item auto-scan array set to [x]-second scan speed with [CVI adaptations], [Student] will activate [switch] to select a target item from a field of [number] when prompted by a natural environmental cue, with 80% accuracy across 3 consecutive sessions, as measured by direct observation data.*

### Stage 4 – Two-Switch Step Scanning

*Given a two-switch setup (Switch 1 = advance, Switch 2 = select) and an [app/AAC device] with [number]-item array, [Student] will advance to and select a named target item using step scanning with 80% first-attempt accuracy across 3 consecutive sessions in at least 2 different settings, as measured by collected trial data.*

### Stage 5 – Multi-Item / AAC Integration

*Given [AAC app/device] with [number]-item grid using row-column scanning at [x]-second scan speed, [Student] will navigate to and select vocabulary items to produce [single-word / 2-symbol] messages to communicate [wants/needs/comments] during [activity/setting] with 80% accuracy over 3 consecutive sessions, as measured by SLP/teacher data.*

### Progress Monitoring Goal (Generalization)

*After meeting stage criterion in structured trials, [Student] will independently initiate switch access to communicate or control [activity] in [at least 2 natural settings] with no more than a verbal prompt, across 5 consecutive observation opportunities per setting, as measured by staff data across settings.*

## APPENDIX E: Implementation in Natural Settings

### Classroom Implementation

- Identify at least one naturally occurring switch opportunity in each relevant routine.
- Keep the switch mounted consistently when possible.
- Train staff to recognize the student's latency and response pattern.
- Avoid moving the switch without documenting the new position.
- Record meaningful functional outcomes, not only switch presses.

### Home Implementation

Home routines should use the same access method when feasible but should not become a second therapy session. Examples include requesting music, controlling a preferred activity, calling a caregiver, participating in meal routines, or selecting entertainment.

### Community and Transition

As skills become reliable, assess access in less controlled environments. Noise, lighting, people moving, transportation, device battery, cable management, and mounting stability can all change performance.

### Staff Training Checklist

- Staff can identify the correct switch and access site.
- Staff can reproduce the established mounting position.
- Staff know the student's natural cue.
- Staff can wait through the documented latency before prompting.
- Staff use the agreed prompt hierarchy.
- Staff record errors and relevant conditions.
- Staff know how to troubleshoot basic connection and battery issues.
- Staff know when to stop and seek OT/PT/AT assistance for positioning or safety concerns.

## APPENDIX F: Troubleshooting Guide

| Problem | Check first | Possible modification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No response | Positioning, switch connection, force, motivation, latency | Reposition, reduce force, increase wait time, retest preference |
| Too many accidental activations | Switch sensitivity, movement path, mounting | Increase specificity, move switch, use different sensor |
| Student hits switch but misses target | Scan speed, target size, visual latency | Slow scan, enlarge target, simplify array |
| Student becomes fatigued | Session length, posture, visual demand | Shorten trials, rest, adjust seating, reduce visual complexity |
| Student responds only with adult prompt | Prompt timing and natural cue | Delay prompt, fade hierarchy, strengthen meaningful consequence |
| Performance changes after mounting | Switch angle and distance | Reproduce measured access geometry |
| CVI student does not look at target | Color, visual field, complexity, distance | Return to simpler display and preferred field/color |
| AAC errors increase with larger grid | Motor and visual complexity | Reduce grid size or use hierarchical scanning |
| Staff report inconsistent performance | Environmental variation | Standardize critical parameters and collect contextual data |

## APPENDIX G: Safety, Ethics, and Clinical Boundaries

### Physical Safety

> **Warning:**
>
> - Do not force a movement that causes pain, abnormal strain, or unsafe posture.
> - Do not alter wheelchair or seating components outside the evaluator's competence.
> - Keep cables and mounting hardware away from entanglement hazards.
> - Inspect switches and mounts routinely for damage.
> - Follow manufacturer instructions for electrical and battery-powered equipment.

### Ethical Access

The purpose of switch access is functional participation and control. Preferred stimulation should not be used to coerce participation or to withhold essential care. Communication opportunities should include rejection and protest as well as requesting.

### Interdisciplinary Scope

OT/PT should address positioning, movement, range, and physical access. SLP/AAC professionals should address communication and language access. TVIs and CVI specialists should address visual access. AT specialists integrate the access system across person, environment, task, and technology.
